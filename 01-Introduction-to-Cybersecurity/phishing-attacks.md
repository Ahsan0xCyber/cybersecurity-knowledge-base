# Phishing Attacks

## 1. What Is Phishing?

Phishing is a social engineering attack in which an attacker sends fraudulent emails, messages, or other communications to trick users into revealing sensitive information, clicking malicious links, or downloading harmful attachments.

### Common Targets
- Usernames and passwords
- Banking and financial information
- Personal and confidential data
- Authentication credentials

## 2. Types of Phishing Attacks

### 2.1 Credential Phishing
Attempts to steal usernames, passwords, or authentication codes through deceptive messages or fake login pages.

### 2.2 Financial Phishing
Targets banking details, payment information, and other financial data.

### 2.3 Spear Phishing
A targeted attack designed for a specific person or organization using personalized information.

### 2.4 Whaling
A type of targeted phishing that focuses on high-profile individuals, such as executives and senior managers.

### 2.5 Malware-Based Phishing
Uses malicious links or email attachments to trick users into downloading or executing harmful software.

### 2.6 Business Email Compromise (BEC)
An attacker impersonates or compromises a trusted business email account to deceive employees into transferring money or disclosing sensitive information.

### 2.7 Smishing and Vishing
- **Smishing:** Phishing through SMS or text messages.
- **Vishing:** Phishing through voice calls.

## 3. How to Identify a Phishing Email

Use the **A-L-L-G checklist**.

- **A — Address:** Check the sender's complete email address and domain.
- **L — Link:** Inspect links carefully; displayed text may hide a different destination.
- **L — Language:** Look for spelling mistakes, unusual wording, threats, or urgent demands.
- **G — Graphics:** Check for inconsistent logos, branding, and email design.

### Common Warning Signs
- Urgent messages such as "Your account will be locked!"
- Pressure to act immediately.
- Suspicious links or unexpected attachments.
- Generic greetings such as "Dear Customer."
- Requests for passwords, authentication codes, or financial information.
- Sender addresses that imitate legitimate organizations.

**Memory Trick:** Fear + Urgency + Suspicious Link = Strong Warning Sign.

A single warning sign does not prove an email is phishing. Verify the sender and message independently.

## 4. Phishing Attack Lifecycle

1. **Targeting:** The attacker selects a person or organization.
2. **Luring:** A deceptive email or message is sent.
3. **Interaction:** The recipient clicks a link, opens an attachment, or shares information.
4. **Compromise:** The attacker may obtain credentials, access, or sensitive data.
5. **Impact:** The incident may cause account compromise, financial loss, or data exposure.

## 5. Indicators of Compromise (IoCs)

Potential indicators associated with phishing include:

- Suspicious sender addresses or lookalike domains.
- Links pointing to unexpected destinations.
- Unexpected or suspicious attachments.
- Unusual sign-in activity after suspected credential theft.
- Unauthorized account activity.
- Unexpected password-reset or authentication notifications.

**Note:** These indicators require investigation; none alone confirms a phishing attack.

## 6. Prevention and Protection

- Verify unexpected requests through a separate trusted channel.
- Check sender addresses and link destinations carefully.
- Never share passwords or multi-factor authentication (MFA) codes.
- Enable MFA to add an extra layer of account protection.
- Keep operating systems, browsers, and security software updated.
- Use email filtering and anti-phishing protection.
- Report suspicious messages through the appropriate reporting process.

## 7. Response to a Suspected Phishing Attack

- Do not click suspicious links or open unexpected attachments.
- Report the message to the appropriate security team or email provider.
- If credentials may have been exposed, change them through the official website.
- Review account activity and revoke suspicious sessions where appropriate.
- Follow the organization's incident-response procedures.

## 8. Real-World Case Study: Razer Data Breach

- **Incident:** A cloud misconfiguration exposed some of Razer's customer-related information.
- **Discovery:** A security researcher identified the exposure.
- **Risk:** Exposed information could potentially support phishing or social engineering.
- **Security Lesson:** Cloud resources must be securely configured and continuously monitored.

### Key Takeaways
- Review cloud permissions and configurations regularly.
- Prevent unauthorized public access to sensitive data.
- Monitor cloud resources for security misconfigurations.
- Respond promptly to reported vulnerabilities and data exposures.

**Important:** This case concerns cloud misconfiguration and data exposure, not a phishing attack itself.

## Key Takeaway

**Stop. Think. Verify.**

Check the sender, inspect links, protect sensitive information, and verify unexpected requests before taking action.
